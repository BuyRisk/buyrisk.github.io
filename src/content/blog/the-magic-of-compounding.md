Compound growth is the closest thing investing has to a superpower, and yet it
looks unremarkable for a long time. Understanding *why* it stays quiet and then
gets loud is one of the most useful things a new investor can learn.

## Interest on interest

Simple interest pays you only on your original deposit. Compound interest pays
you on your deposit **and** on all the interest you've already earned. Each
period's gain becomes next period's base.

The formula for a single lump sum is:

```text
Future value = Principal × (1 + r) ^ years
```

where `r` is the annual growth rate. That little exponent is where the magic
hides. Growth stacks on growth.

## Why it feels slow, then fast

Suppose you invest $10,000 at 7% per year.

| Year | Balance   |
| ---- | --------- |
| 1    | $10,700   |
| 5    | $14,026   |
| 10   | $19,672   |
| 20   | $38,697   |
| 30   | $76,123   |

The *first* decade adds under $10,000. The *third* decade adds more than
$37,000, on the same original deposit and the same rate. Nothing changed
except that the base got bigger. That acceleration is the whole point.

## The rule of 72

Want a quick estimate of how long money takes to double? Divide 72 by the
annual return:

- At 6%, money doubles in about **12 years** (72 ÷ 6).
- At 9%, about **8 years** (72 ÷ 9).

It's not exact, but it's close enough to do in your head, and it makes the cost
of a lower return vivid.
